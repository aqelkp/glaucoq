Basic Level Basic Science · Q1 10 Marks

Describe with figures the anatomy of the anterior chamber, angle, and ciliary body.

1. Anatomy of the Anterior Chamber (AC)

The anterior chamber is an aqueous humor-filled space located between the cornea anteriorly and the iris and pupillary portion of the crystalline lens posteriorly, while its periphery is bounded by the iridocorneal (anterior chamber) angle .

In a normal adult eye, the central anterior chamber depth measures approximately 3.0 mm (average range 3.0–3.15 mm; overall 2.5–4.4 mm), the horizontal diameter is 11.3–12.4 mm, and the volume is approximately 200–250 µL (commonly cited as ~220 µL), decreasing slightly with age by about 0.11 µL per year .

Clinical Correlation: The anterior chamber is physiologically deeper in myopia, aphakia, and pseudophakia (PCIOL), whereas it is shallower in hypermetropia, intumescent cataract, and primary angle closure (PAC) .

2. Anatomy of the Anterior Chamber Angle

The anterior chamber angle lies at the junction of the peripheral cornea, sclera, ciliary body, and iris root, and plays a crucial role in conventional aqueous humor drainage . Because of total internal reflection at the tear–air interface, the angle cannot be viewed directly and must be visualized clinically by gonioscopy .

From anterior to posterior, the four major gonioscopic landmarks are remembered by the mnemonic S–T–S–C: Schwalbe's line, Trabecular meshwork, Scleral spur, and Ciliary body band (followed by the iris root) .

A. Schwalbe's Line

Schwalbe's line is a fine, glistening white ridge representing the peripheral termination of Descemet's membrane and the transition zone between the corneal endothelium and the trabecular meshwork endothelium . Anatomically, it forms the anterior border of both the trabecular meshwork and the internal scleral sulcus, and may be non-pigmented or lightly pigmented .

Gonioscopic & Clinical Correlations:
  • Corneal Wedge: On slit-lamp gonioscopy, Schwalbe's line is precisely localized at the apex where the anterior and posterior corneal light beams converge .
  • Sampaolesi Line: A wavy line of pigment deposited on or anterior to Schwalbe's line, classically seen in pseudoexfoliation syndrome (PXF) and pigment dispersion syndrome (PDS) .
  • Posterior Embryotoxon: A prominent, anteriorly displaced Schwalbe's line visible on slit-lamp examination; it occurs in up to 15% of normal eyes and is strongly associated with Axenfeld–Rieger syndrome and Alagille syndrome .

B. Trabecular Meshwork (TM) & Schlemm's Canal

The trabecular meshwork is a porous, sieve-like, contractile connective tissue structure lined by trabeculocytes that bridges the internal scleral sulcus and converts it into a circular channel, Schlemm's canal . On cross-section, the TM is triangular in shape with its apex at Schwalbe's line and its base at the scleral spur and anterior ciliary body .

Gonioscopically, the TM is divided into two zones: the anterior non-pigmented trabecular meshwork (ATM), which is largely non-functional, and the posterior pigmented trabecular meshwork (PTM), which overlies Schlemm's canal and represents the primary site of aqueous humor filtration . Histologically, from inside (anterior chamber) to outside (Schlemm's canal), it consists of three distinct layers :

  1. Uveal Trabecular Meshwork: This is the innermost layer facing the anterior chamber, extending from the iris root and ciliary body to Schwalbe's line . It is 2–3 layers thick and composed of cord-like trabecular beams with large, irregular openings measuring 25–75 µm, therefore offering minimal resistance to aqueous outflow .
  2. Corneoscleral Trabecular Meshwork: This forms the largest middle portion, extending from the scleral spur to the anterior wall of the scleral sulcus . It consists of multiple flattened, perforated sheets of trabeculae whose elliptical openings become progressively smaller toward Schlemm's canal, measuring 5–50 µm .
  3. Juxtacanalicular (Cribriform) Tissue (JCT): This outermost layer is 2–5 cell layers thick and lies directly adjacent to the inner wall endothelium of Schlemm's canal, with the narrowest openings measuring 2–20 µm . Together with the inner wall of Schlemm's canal, it forms the primary site of aqueous outflow resistance owing to its narrow, tortuous pathway and dense extracellular matrix .

Schlemm's Canal & Collector Channels: Schlemm's canal is a 360° endothelial-lined channel within the scleral sulcus whose inner wall contains giant vacuoles and pores that transmit aqueous into the lumen . From Schlemm's canal, aqueous drains through 25–35 collector channels into the intrascleral venous plexus, aqueous veins, episcleral veins, and eventually into the cavernous sinus .

Age-Related Changes in the TM: Aging leads to (i) increased pigment accumulation, (ii) loss of trabecular endothelial cells, (iii) thickening of basement membranes, and (iv) accumulation of cellular debris and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in the extracellular space—all of which increase outflow resistance and predispose to primary open-angle glaucoma .

C. Scleral Spur

The scleral spur is the most anterior inward projection of the sclera, composed of a circumferential ridge of collagen fibers (the scleral roll) that forms the posterior border of the internal scleral sulcus . On gonioscopy, it appears as a sharp, prominent white line immediately posterior to the trabecular meshwork . Functionally, it serves as the anterior site of attachment for the longitudinal fibers of the ciliary muscle (Brücke's muscle); contraction of this muscle pulls the scleral spur posteriorly, opening the intertrabecular spaces and increasing aqueous outflow .

D. Ciliary Body Band (CBB)

The ciliary body band is the most posterior landmark visible in the angle recess on gonioscopy, appearing as a dull grey to dark brown band representing the anterior face of the longitudinal ciliary muscle lying between the scleral spur and the iris root . Its visible width depends on the level of iris insertion—it is typically wider in myopes and aphakic eyes and narrower in hypermetropic eyes . It also serves as the entry site for the uveoscleral (unconventional) outflow pathway .

Blunt Trauma Correlations:
  • Angle Recession: A tear into the anterior face of the ciliary body between the longitudinal and circular muscle fibers widens the CBB and causes increased IOP (angle recession glaucoma) .
  • Cyclodialysis Cleft: Complete separation of the ciliary body from the scleral spur creates a direct communication with the suprachoroidal space, causing profound decreased IOP (hypotony) .

3. Anatomy of the Ciliary Body

The ciliary body is the middle portion of the uveal tract that forms a complete 6 mm ring extending anteriorly from the scleral spur and iris root to the ora serrata posteriorly . In meridional cross-section, it is shaped like a right-angled triangle whose base faces anteriorly toward the iris and whose apex tapers posteriorly to join the choroid . Its total anteroposterior length measures 4.6–5.2 mm nasally and 5.6–6.3 mm temporally .

A. Gross Subdivisions

  • Pars Plicata (Corona Ciliaris): The anterior, ridged portion measuring 1.5–2.0 mm in length (~25% of the ciliary body). It contains 70–80 radial major ciliary processes interspersed with minor processes, which provide a large surface area for aqueous humor secretion and attachment of the lens zonules .
  • Pars Plana (Orbicularis Ciliaris): The posterior, smooth, heavily pigmented zone measuring 3.5–4.5 mm in length (~75% of the ciliary body), extending to the ora serrata . Because it is relatively avascular, it serves as the standard safe entry site for intravitreal injections and pars plana vitrectomy (3.0–4.0 mm posterior to the surgical limbus) .

B. Histological Layers

From inside (vitreous/posterior chamber) to outside (sclera), the ciliary body is organized into three layers :

  1. Ciliary Epithelium: A double layer of neuroectodermal cells aligned apex-to-apex. The outer pigmented ciliary epithelium (PCE) is continuous with the retinal pigment epithelium, while the inner non-pigmented ciliary epithelium (NPE) faces the posterior chamber and is continuous with the neurosensory retina . Tight junctions (zonulae occludentes) between adjacent NPE cells constitute the blood–aqueous barrier and actively secrete aqueous humor via Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and carbonic anhydrase .
  2. Ciliary Stroma: Loose connective tissue containing a rich network of fenestrated capillaries supplied by the major arterial circle of the iris (formed by the anterior ciliary arteries and long posterior ciliary arteries) .
  3. Ciliary Muscle: A smooth muscle innervated by parasympathetic fibers of CN III (Edinger–Westphal nucleus) comprising three orientations: outer longitudinal fibers (Brücke's muscle) inserting into the scleral spur, intermediate radial (oblique) fibers, and inner circular fibers (Müller's muscle) responsible for accommodation .

Describe the anatomy of the retinal nerve fiber layer, optic nerve head, with figures and visual pathway from the retina to the visual cortex.

1. Anatomy of the Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer (RNFL)

The retinal nerve fiber layer is the innermost neural layer of the retina, lying directly beneath the internal limiting membrane and composed of approximately 1.2 million (range 1.2–1.5 million) unmyelinated axons of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) converging toward the optic nerve head . These axons are organized into bundles separated by Müller cell processes and are thickest at the superior and inferior poles of the optic disc .

Topographically, the RNFL axons follow three distinct patterns across the fundus :

  • Papillomacular Bundle: Fibers originating from the fovea and macula travel directly horizontally into the temporal aspect of the optic disc.
  • Superior and Inferior Arcuate Bundles: Fibers arising temporal to the macula arch around the papillomacular bundle to enter the superior and inferior poles of the disc without crossing the temporal horizontal raphe .
  • Nasal Radiating Fibers: Fibers arising from the nasal retina take a direct, radial course into the nasal margin of the optic disc.
Glaucoma Correlation: Because arcuate fibers do not cross the horizontal raphe and enter the most vulnerable superotemporal and inferotemporal lamina cribrosa pores, early glaucomatous damage produces paracentral scotomas, arcuate (Bjerrum) scotomas, and nasal steps that strictly respect the horizontal meridian .

2. Anatomy of the Optic Nerve Head (ONH)

The optic nerve head (optic disc) is the intraocular site where RGC axons converge and turn 90° posteriorly to exit the globe through the scleral canal . It measures approximately 1.5 mm in diameter (slightly oval vertically) and displays a central pale depression—the physiologic optic cup—surrounded by a pink neuroretinal rim . In healthy eyes, neuroretinal rim width typically follows the ISNT rule (Inferior ≥ Superior ≥ Nasal ≥ Temporal), although normal anatomic variants exist . The collagenous Border Tissue of Elschnig separates the choroid and Bruch's membrane from the optic nerve neural tissue at the disc margin .

Histologically, the optic nerve head is divided into four anatomical zones from anterior to posterior :

  1. Surface Nerve Fiber Layer: Composed of unmyelinated RGC axons and astrocytes; its capillary bed is derived primarily from branches of the central retinal artery (CRA) .
  2. Prelaminar Region: Lies at the level of the choroid, where axon bundles are separated by astroglial columns; it receives its blood supply mainly from the peripapillary choroidal vessels (via short posterior ciliary arteries) .
  3. Laminar Region (Lamina Cribrosa): A sieve-like meshwork of stacked, fenestrated collagenous beams spanning the posterior scleral canal through which axon bundles pass . It is supplied by the anastomotic Circle of Zinn–Haller (derived from the short posterior ciliary arteries) and represents the primary site of glaucomatous axonal injury, where elevated IOP and translaminar pressure gradients cause posterior bowing and blockade of axoplasmic transport .
  4. Retrolaminar Region: Posterior to the lamina cribrosa, the RGC axons acquire myelin sheaths produced by oligodendrocytes, doubling the optic nerve diameter to ~3 mm, and become encased by the pia, arachnoid, and dura mater .

3. Visual Pathway from Retina to Visual Cortex

The afferent visual pathway conducts visual impulses from the retinal photoreceptors to the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe via a three-neuron chain: photoreceptors synapse on bipolar cells (1st-order), which synapse on retinal ganglion cells (2nd-order), whose axons project to the lateral geniculate nucleus to synapse on geniculocalcarine neurons (3rd-order) .

Anatomical Segment Detailed Anatomy & Clinical Localizing Value
1. Optic Nerve Measures ~50 mm in total and is divided into four segments: intraocular (1 mm), intraorbital (25 mm), intracanalicular (9 mm), and intracranial (16 mm). Unilateral lesions cause ipsilateral monocular visual loss and an afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) .
2. Optic Chiasm Located above the sella turcica; here, nasal retinal fibers (~53%) decussate to the contralateral optic tract while temporal retinal fibers remain uncrossed. Midline chiasmal compression classically produces bitemporal hemianopia .
3. Optic Tract Extends posteriorly from the chiasm carrying ipsilateral temporal and contralateral nasal retinal fibers (representing the contralateral hemifield). Pupillary afferent fibers exit before the LGN to reach the pretectal nucleus. Lesions cause incongruous contralateral homonymous hemianopia .
4. Lateral Geniculate Nucleus (LGN) A six-layered thalamic relay nucleus: ventral Layers 1–2 are Magnocellular (M) (motion, contrast, low spatial frequency) and dorsal Layers 3–6 are Parvocellular (P) (color, fine detail, high spatial frequency). Crossed nasal fibers synapse in layers 1, 4, and 6, whereas uncrossed temporal fibers synapse in layers 2, 3, and 5 .
5. Optic Radiations (Geniculocalcarine Tract) Third-order axons fan out into two divisions: inferior fibers sweep anteriorly into the temporal lobe as Meyer's loop (carrying superior visual field → temporal lesions cause contralateral superior quadrantanopia, "pie in the sky"), while superior fibers pass through the parietal lobe as Baum's loop (carrying inferior visual field → parietal lesions cause contralateral inferior quadrantanopia, "pie on the floor") .
6. Primary Visual Cortex (V1 / Area 17) Situated on the medial surface of the occipital lobe along the upper and lower banks of the calcarine sulcus. The macula has a disproportionately large representation at the posterior occipital pole with dual blood supply (PCA and MCA), explaining macular sparing in congruous homonymous hemianopia from posterior cerebral artery occlusion .
